Job market paper

Healthcare Privatization and Patient Health: Evidence from Puerto Rico

Paper (PDF)

Public health systems increasingly buy care from private providers: the U.S. Veterans Health Administration more than doubled such spending in 2014–2021 (Congressional Budget Office, 2021), New Zealand’s private share of publicly funded elective procedures nearly doubled from 2011–12 to 2023–24 (Radio New Zealand, 2026), and England’s health department bought £24.1 billion of care outside the NHS in 2024–25 (National Audit Office, 2026). Yet little is known about how patients fare when a publicly financed health system moves entirely from public provision to private management. To study this shift at full scale, we examine Puerto Rico’s 1994–2000 health reform, which changed facility ownership, provider payment incentives, eligibility, and access. Exploiting the reform’s staggered rollout across counties, we find that it raised all-cause mortality by 3.7 percent. The increase is accompanied by lower ambulatory-care employment, fewer hospitals, and lower use of early prenatal care, consistent with reduced provider capacity and access to timely care as possible mechanisms. These findings suggest that a broad shift toward privately managed care may harm patient health.

Working papers

Corporate Tax Incentives Retraction: Evidence from Puerto Rico

Paper (PDF)

We know much about the effects of introducing place-based tax incentives for corporations. Given how costly these incentives are, we know surprisingly little about their effects once they are retracted. To fill this gap, this paper studies the retraction of one of the costliest corporate tax incentives in the United States, Section 936 of the U.S. Internal Revenue Code. This incentive exempted U.S. corporations from federal tax on income earned in Puerto Rico from 1976 until Congress phased it out beginning in 1996 and eliminated it in 2006. Manufacturing claimed 98 percent of the tax credits and produced a median 45.5 percent of island GDP at the time, making it one of the most important industries in the economy. Using synthetic difference-in-differences on employment data for Puerto Rico and U.S. states, this paper estimates that manufacturing employment fell 9.2 percent by 2000, 13.3 percent by the close of the phase-out in 2005, and 28.0 percent by 2019, relative to the counterfactual. Results show no return to baseline employment levels two decades later. The average county-level decline is 13.6 percent, and reaches 18.2 percent in high-poverty counties. This evidence leads us to conclude that even after three decades of tax subsidy, the retraction of place-based tax incentives fails to ignite self-sustaining industries.

Work in progress

Physician Migration from Puerto Rico and the Health of Receiving Communities

with Jori Barash (University of Oregon)

Does adding physicians improve health where they arrive? Physicians choose where to practice, so comparisons across communities can confound the effects of physician supply with differences in local demand. We study the physicians who left Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria in September 2017 and propose each mainland county’s 2010 share of residents born in Puerto Rico as an instrument for where they settled. Outcomes include hospital use, preventable hospitalizations, access to care, and mortality from 2012 to 2022. We also ask whether effects are larger for Hispanic patients, as an indicator of homophily.
